# Provenance IDs need the codes watermarking skipped

Error-correcting codes are a thin layer in image watermarking, and thinnest exactly where the proposed study sits: post-hoc neural watermarks that must return an exact identifier. Classical work (roughly 1999–2005) found that soft-decoded LDPC and block turbo codes about doubled payload over BCH at fixed PSNR, but also that at fixed image quality every parity bit dilutes embedding energy; since then ECC terms have appeared in a flat 1–2% of image-watermarking papers per year. Among post-hoc learned watermarks only a minority ship a code, and the prominent ones (StegaStamp, TrustMark, InvisMark) all use hard-decision BCH on 100–256 bits, while Meta's whole line, including the 256-bit Pixel Seal and the 1024-bit ChunkySeal, uses none and reports bit accuracy and p-values; soft-decoded LDPC appears mainly in in-generation diffusion watermarks. Industry codes the payload when an identifier must be recovered exactly (TrustMark, InvisMark, Digimarc) and skips coding for presence flags, and the C2PA standard leaves payload size, ECC and false-positive control entirely to vendors. No benchmark requires exact recovery of a multi-bit ID, exact-recovery metrics get reinvented paper by paper, and wrong-ID rates are almost never reported. The proposed study is worth pursuing: no located work applies the same modern codes to classical and post-hoc neural backbones at matched quality, feeds neural extractor logits to LDPC or polar decoders, or measures known-header decoding against wrong-ID rates. Its novelty lies in that systematic exact-ID evaluation rather than in any single code, and the window is narrowing, with two closely related preprints in September–October 2026 and an LDPC-coded watermark at CVPR 2026.

## Classical watermarking proved soft codes work, then moved on

The classical literature treated the watermark as a very noisy channel. Darbon, Sankur and Maître reported raw error rates "between 0.1 and 0.5" as "not uncommon". They showed that BCH codes from (127,64) to (1023,66) "fail for channels worse than pb = 0.1", after which they produce more errors than no coding at all. A (32,1) repetition code overtook BCH once raw BER passed **about 0.15–0.20** ([Darbon et al. 2001](https://lre.epita.fr/dload/papers/darbon.01.ei.pdf)). Desset, Macq and Vandendorpe stated the design rule that dominated the period: "The repetition code lowers the huge channel BER, as no other code can do this part of the job", and an algebraic code then cleans up what remains ([Desset et al. 2002](https://dial.uclouvain.be/pr/boreal/object/boreal:41836)).

Classical systems were therefore concatenations. An inner repetition or spreading stage pushes the per-coded-bit BER under the outer code's threshold, and BCH, RS or convolutional codes sit on top. Multi-code comparisons were common, but always among the older families. Abdul, Carré and Gaborit compared repetition, Hamming, BCH and RS in colour wavelet watermarking ([Abdul et al. 2013](https://jis-eurasipjournals.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/1687-417X-2013-1)). An IEICE report compared BCH, RS, Sourlas and LDPC codes and found BCH plus LDPC the best concatenation ([IEICE 2015](https://www.ieice.org/ken/paper/201501293Bvd/eng/)).

Soft iterative codes produced the largest measured gains whenever someone tried them. Bastug and Sankur fixed strength at about 38 dB PSNR and extracted each coded bit with a soft maximum-likelihood detector. Reaching BER 1e-3 needed a chip rate of 96 with LDPC, 176 with BCH and 256 with pure repetition. That is **about twice BCH's payload and 2.7 times repetition's, a 3 dB advantage**. Their 256-bit messages survived JPEG down to Q = 70 with LDPC, against Q = 85 for BCH ([Bastug & Sankur](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1343/download/cm-bastah-040201.pdf)).

Rey et al. ran a BCH product code with Chase–Pyndiah iterative decoding over soft repetition on 75 images at 38 dB. They decoded a 121-bit payload without error down to **JPEG 30%, while repetition alone failed at 50%** ([Rey et al. 2003](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1360/download/mm-reych-030121.pdf)). Hirata et al. later fed weighted majority votes into an (800,200) PEG-LDPC code. They reached zero BER at 41.1 dB under the IHC v4 attack set, correcting channel error rates up to 15% ([Hirata et al. 2017](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/transinf/E100.D/1/E100.D_2016MUP0003/_pdf)). These papers are explicit that the soft input carries the gain: "When the repetition codes are hard-decoded, the performances of the turbo decoder decrease significantly" ([Rey et al. 2003](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1360/download/mm-reych-030121.pdf)).

They are equally explicit about the cost. At fixed distortion, a longer codeword means fewer repetitions per coded bit. Rey et al. found that a longer product code's "coding gain is not sufficient to compensate the loss due to the decrease in the replication factor L which is here divided by 4". They bounded the replication factor in a 512×512 image at **L < 57 for a 256-bit codeword and L < 15 for a 1024-bit one** ([Rey et al. 2003](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1360/download/mm-reych-030121.pdf)). Darbon et al. insisted that Eb/N0 "must be scaled by k/n for a fair comparison". They compared codes at an equal number of pixels per bit ([Darbon et al. 2001](https://lre.epita.fr/dload/papers/darbon.01.ei.pdf)).

A sceptical camp went further. Gu, Huang and Shi argued that "the redundancy introduced by ECC will lead to a decrease of the watermark strength". They concluded that ECC "cannot effectively improve the robustness of watermarking against most attacks", with cropping and jitter the exceptions ([Gu, Huang & Shi 2003](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/analysis-of-the-role-played-by-error-correcting-coding-in-robust-/); [Huang, Gu & Shi 2005](https://digitalcommons.njit.edu/fac_pubs/19584)). The two camps mostly disagree about normalisation and decoder input. Studies that held the embedding budget fixed and decoded softly found gains. The sceptics argued from lost strength, and their normalisation could not be checked because the full texts were unavailable.

Theory says the headroom is large. Chen and Wornell placed uncoded binary spread-transform dither modulation **about 13.6 dB from capacity**. They wrote that "at least 9.3 dB" of that gap could be recovered through channel coding, vector quantisation and non-dithered quantisation. Closing about 6 dB with practical codes they called "fairly easy" ([Chen & Wornell 2001](https://dsp-group.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/QuantizationIndexMod.pdf)). The `dwtDctSvd` embedder in the invisible-watermark library is, in those terms, uncoded binary scalar quantisation-index modulation plus repetition. It quantises the top singular value of each 4×4 block and averages hard per-block decisions into each bit ([invisible-watermark](https://github.com/ShieldMnt/invisible-watermark)). Informed (dirty-paper) coding went further still. Miller, Doërr and Cox carried **1380 bits in 240×368 images with 0% message error**, at roughly half the perceptual distance at which blind coding failed 99.85% of the time ([Miller, Doërr & Cox 2004](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1414/download/mm-doergw-040601.pdf)). That route, however, requires changing the embedder.

Codes also left one problem untouched. They correct substitutions, not the insertions and deletions that desynchronisation creates. Coumou and Sharma called synchronisation "the Achilles heel of a majority of current watermarking systems" ([Coumou & Sharma 2006](https://www.cecs.uci.edu/~papers/icme06/pdfs/0000849.pdf)). In practice, image systems handled geometric attacks with templates and invariant domains rather than with codes.

Then the field moved on. The work clusters around 1999–2005; this is a reading of the sources' publication dates, not a measured trend. It was followed by sporadic applied papers and by a 2022 book chapter that treats robust watermarking as "the classical application framework for the use of codes in watermarking" ([Lefèvre, Carré & Gaborit 2022](https://scholar.xjtlu.edu.cn/en/publications/codes-and-watermarks/)). OpenAlex keyword counts show the niche's size over the last decade ([OpenAlex API](https://api.openalex.org/works)):

The counts also overstate the field. The second most-cited "ECC" hit since 2020, a TCSVT paper, says it is robust "even without any error correcting codes" ([TCSVT 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/tcsvt.2020.2998476)). Papers whose actual contribution is coding for robust multi-bit image watermarks probably number a few per year.

## Neural watermarks ship hard BCH or no code at all

Of the roughly twenty learned watermarks inventoried for 2018–2026, only a handful protect their payload with an algebraic code. The widely used post-hoc models that ship one all use a short binary BCH decoded with hard decisions:

| System | Raw bits → protected data | Coding | Recovery metric reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| StegaStamp (CVPR 2020) | 100 → 56 | BCH, t = 5 | bit accuracy; failed BCH filters a detection |
| TrustMark (Adobe) | 100 → 40/61/68/75 | BCH t = 8/5/4/3 in code release; none in paper | bit accuracy only |
| InvisMark (Microsoft, WACV 2025) | 256 → 128-bit UUID | BCH, t = 16 | bit accuracy and decoding success rate |
| Distortion-Agnostic (Luo et al., CVPR 2020) | 120 → 30 | learned NECST code, trained separately | bit accuracy |
| Gaussian Shading (CVPR 2024) | 256 | repetition, hard majority vote | bit accuracy, TPR |
| PRC watermark (ICLR 2025) | 512 robust | pseudorandom LDPC, soft BP + OSD | detection TPR, decoding |
| Meta: SSL, Stable Signature, WAM, Video Seal, Pixel Seal, ChunkySeal | 30–1024 | none | bit accuracy, p-value, TPR at fixed FPR |
| HiDDeN, RivaGAN, MBRS, CIN, VINE, EditGuard, AquaLoRA | 30–256 | none (some learned redundancy) | bit accuracy / BER |

StegaStamp reserves 40 of its 100 bits for BCH and "filter[s] out the proposal" when correction fails ([StegaStamp](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.05343)). TrustMark's repository offers four BCH schemas over a 96-bit field ([adobe/trustmark](https://github.com/adobe/trustmark)). Its paper states, "We do not apply such correction in any of our reported results" ([TrustMark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18297)). InvisMark carries "128-bit UUID and error correction bits" in 256 ([InvisMark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07795)), with `bchlib` at t = 16 in its code ([microsoft/InvisMark](https://github.com/microsoft/InvisMark)).

**Meta's watermarking line never uses an ECC.** Video Seal critiques bit accuracy as "agnostic to the number of bits being hidden" and replaces it with a binomial p-value and an "expected capacity" C(p) = nbits·(1 − h(p)), not with coding ([Video Seal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09492)). Pixel Seal's extractor "outputs a 'soft' message m̃ ∈ [0,1]^nbits which is thresholded" ([Pixel Seal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16874)). That soft output is exactly what an LDPC or polar decoder would want, and the paper throws it away.

ChunkySeal frames capacity geometrically, by counting the images that satisfy PSNR and robustness constraints, and declines the Gel'fand–Pinsker channel-coding framing. It reports only bit accuracy: **99.15% overall at 1024 bits and 45.32 dB**, with cliffs at 55% crop (86.90%), 10% brightness (83.62%) and 30° rotation (49.56%) ([ChunkySeal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12812)). The `videoseal` repository that hosts all three models contains no BCH, Reed–Solomon or LDPC code ([facebookresearch/videoseal](https://github.com/facebookresearch/videoseal)).

The field's orthodoxy is that ECC is orthogonal and can be added later. HiDDeN wrote, "One can use error correcting codes if higher accuracy is desired" ([HiDDeN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.09937)). VINE says BCH "can be applied to all watermarking models" and excludes it from its baselines ([VINE / W-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18775)). The stated objections are pragmatic rather than principled. Luo et al. rejected LDPC because "traditional codes such as LDPC require the decoder to have an estimate of the channel noise strength" ([Luo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04580)). DREW argues that 100–200 bits leave too little room "for effective error correction" ([DREW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02836)). A 2026 survey concludes that "only a few recent methods have incorporated it" because adding redundancy "further reduces the usable payload" ([Cao et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02384)).

Where codes have been measured on learned watermarks, three patterns appear. First, **hard BCH produces a cliff**. In RoSteALS's table, StegaStamp's 0.88 bit accuracy becomes **0.48 ± 0.50** after BCH, SSL's 0.62 becomes 0.03, and `dwtDctSvd`'s 0.61 becomes 0.16. Each image either decodes perfectly or fails ([RoSteALS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03400)). InvisMark's 97.7% bit accuracy on DALL·E 3 images under JPEG becomes 92.7% exact UUID recovery ([InvisMark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07795)). A 2026 payload-matched study on MBRS goes further: past the correction radius, "hard-decision decoding does not fail — it miscorrects", corrupting **about 48% of the block's message bits** ([Li, Zenodo 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/22970405)).

Second, **raw bits are not free for a network**. In Luo et al.'s ablation, adding a rate-1/4 code to an adversarially trained model lowered JPEG-50 bit accuracy from 85.0% to 81.7%, because the network had to embed four times as many bits ([Luo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04580)).

Third, **iterative soft-decodable codes beat BCH wherever the two were compared head to head**. Gaussian Shading++ swapped its pseudorandom LDPC header code for BCH. TPR at 10⁻⁶ FPR fell from 0.974 to 0.588 under JPEG and from 0.974 to 0.008 under Gaussian blur. Soft-versus-hard decoding added smaller gains, for example 0.966 to 0.974 under JPEG ([Gaussian Shading++](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15026)). A 2026 training-free latent scheme raised exact extraction on its hardest attack from 81.8% to 91.7% by adding Chase decoding on top of BCH ([Sign Clamping](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40204)). One exception points the other way: a Frontiers 2026 comparison ranked BCH (0.9641) above LDPC (0.9540), but it measured bit accuracy only and reported no exact-recovery rate ([Hu et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physics/articles/10.3389/fphy.2025.1750515/full)).

The modern codes therefore live mostly in *in-generation* diffusion watermarks, where the "channel" is the error from inverting the diffusion sampler. PRC decodes 512 bits robustly with soft belief propagation (BP) plus ordered-statistics decoding (OSD) ([PRC watermark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07369)). Gaussian Shannon pairs a (3,4)-regular LDPC code with majority voting at 256 bits ([Gaussian Shannon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26167)). Post-hoc work is sparser. DREW wraps TrustMark with a successive-cancellation polar decoder and reports retrieval gains of up to 40% ([DREW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02836)). QRMark uses Reed–Solomon to recover Stable Signature's accuracy after tiled decoding drops it from 99.7% to 87.5% ([QRMark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02447)). A template watermark with neural extraction and polar codes carries 650–1600 bits in 1080p images at 39.66 dB, with about 1% extraction error after social-network transfer ([Evsutin et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/5b00baaef8044d8d9e8a2fdd1b4e02ee)).

No located paper trains an image watermark with a differentiable BCH, LDPC or polar decoder in the loop. Luo et al. deliberately decoupled even their learned code, because joint training "results in overfitting and less robustness" ([Luo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04580)).

Citation counts show the same split. The heavily cited papers use ECC incidentally: StegaStamp has 531 OpenAlex citations and Luo et al. 216. The coding-centred papers are lightly cited: InvisMark 30, the PRC watermark 9 (an arXiv undercount), the polar Access paper 4 and Gaussian Shannon 0 ([OpenAlex API](https://api.openalex.org/works)).

LLM text watermarking is now ahead of images on coding rigour. Qu et al. use Reed–Solomon and report a **97.6% exact "match rate"** for 20-bit messages, against 49.2% for an uncoded baseline ([Qu et al., USENIX Security 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16820)). Chao et al. build on LDPC ([Chao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10281)). CORE-BREW derives per-token LLRs for soft list decoding because "existing ECC-based LLM watermarks rely on hard-decision decoding" ([CORE-BREW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24163)).

## Industry codes identifiers, not AI-disclosure flags

The C2PA standard creates the strongest practical demand for exact recovery and then says nothing about how to achieve it. A watermark soft binding is an opaque, algorithm-specific `value`. A manifest is recovered only when "both the algorithm identifier (alg) and the value (value)" match. The spec has **no field or rule for payload length, ECC or detection thresholds**; those live on each algorithm's own information page ([C2PA Spec 2.5](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.5/specs/C2PA_Specification.html)).

The soft-binding algorithm list held 57 entries on 4 November 2026: 48 watermarks, 27 of them for images. **None mentions ECC.** Its inclusion criteria contain no robustness, capacity or false-positive requirement. Google, OpenAI, Amazon and first-party Meta are absent ([SBAL JSON](https://github.com/c2pa-org/softbinding-algorithm-list/blob/main/softbinding-algorithm-list.json); [SBAL README](https://github.com/c2pa-org/softbinding-algorithm-list)). The resolution API does allow `maxResults` candidates with a 0–100 `similarityScore` ([C2PA Soft Binding API 2.5](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.5/softbinding/Decoupled.html)). A resolver can therefore do Hamming-tolerant database matching, which is decoding on the database side instead of in the watermark.

Adobe's TrustMark is the clearest public case of ECC in production use. Its 100 raw bits hold 4 version bits and a 96-bit field coded as BCH_SUPER, BCH_5, BCH_4 or BCH_3. These schemas protect 40, 61, 68 or 75 data bits and correct 8, 5, 4 or 3 flips, and the default is BCH_5 with a 61-bit ID ([TrustMark CONFIG.md](https://github.com/adobe/trustmark/blob/main/python/CONFIG.md)). Adobe sizes the code from measured bit accuracy. About 96% bit accuracy at 42–45 dB means roughly 4 flips per 100 bits, "correctable using BCH error correction at a cost of around 30 bits" ([TrustMark FAQ](https://github.com/adobe/trustmark/blob/main/FAQ.md)).

The reference decoder also uses BCH success as the presence test: `wm_present` is true if and only if decoding succeeds ([datalayer.py](https://github.com/adobe/trustmark/blob/main/python/trustmark/datalayer.py)). By my calculation, a bounded-distance BCH_5 decoder accepts a uniformly random 96-bit word with probability about **1.9×10⁻³**, while BCH_SUPER accepts one with probability about 2×10⁻⁶. ECC-as-detector is therefore weak at the default setting, which is a concrete argument for a separate known-header test.

Other deployed systems split the same way: they code the payload when it is an identifier and skip coding when it is a presence tag.

| System | Raw payload | Coding disclosed | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adobe TrustMark | 100 bits | BCH; default 61-bit ID | C2PA soft-binding key |
| Microsoft InvisMark | 256 bits | BCH; 128-bit UUID | C2PA soft-binding key |
| Digimarc (patents) | 47 data + 24 CRC bits | CRC plus BCH/RS/convolutional/turbo options, ~1024 coded bits, 16-chip spreading | product and C2PA IDs |
| Google SynthID-O | 136 bits | none described | model/customer payload; detection solved separately |
| ImmoStory (Video Seal) | 64-bit constant + 192-bit ID | none stated; header match at ≥56/64 bits | AI marker + manifest key |
| Stable Diffusion 1.x / SDXL (`dwtDct`) | 136 / 48-bit fixed tag | none | presence tag |
| Amazon Titan | undisclosed | undisclosed | presence API |

Digimarc's patents describe a 47-bit payload with 24 CRC bits, coded with "block codes, BCH, Reed Solomon, convolutional codes, turbo codes, etc." to about 1024 bits, and claim the redundancy makes "false decodes essentially a nullity" ([US 11,568,165](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11568165B1/en)). InvisMark joined the C2PA list in October 2025 ([SBAL JSON](https://github.com/c2pa-org/softbinding-algorithm-list/blob/main/softbinding-algorithm-list.json)). Stability's reference pipelines embed a fixed 136-bit string ([CompVis txt2img.py](https://github.com/CompVis/stable-diffusion/blob/main/scripts/txt2img.py)) or a fixed 48-bit tag ([diffusers SDXL watermark.py](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion_xl/watermark.py)) with `dwtDct`, with no code. The library itself warns that it "cannot guarantee to decode the original watermarks 100% accurately even though we don't apply any attack" ([invisible-watermark](https://github.com/ShieldMnt/invisible-watermark)).

Meta's open models reach C2PA only through third parties, each with a raw 256-bit payload. ImmoStory's layout matches the requester's design closely: a public 64-bit constant matched at "56 of these 64 bits" plus a 192-bit random ID that "resolves to the original C2PA manifest", with no ECC stated ([ImmoStory](https://immostory.ai/en/provenance)). By my calculation, that header rule alone gives a false-match probability of about 2.8×10⁻¹⁰ on random bits.

SynthID shows why the split exists. Google reports over **180 billion images and videos** watermarked, with OpenAI, NVIDIA, Kakao and Apple named as partners ([Google, Oct 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/synth-id-ai-content/)). SynthID-Image solves detection and payload "separately". In its worst-case combined attacks, presence detection keeps **98.06% TPR at 0.1% FPR**, while per-bit payload accuracy falls to **89.46%** ([SynthID-Image](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09263)). If those errors were independent, all 136 bits would be correct in about 3 of every 10 million images (my arithmetic). Presence detection needs no code; identifier recovery under strong attacks is impossible without one.

Regulation pulls in both directions. The EU's Code of Practice under AI Act Article 50(2), published 10 June 2026, requires content to be detectable as AI-generated, which is a presence requirement ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content)). Law-firm summaries describe the model identifier as optional and push identifying data into signed metadata ([Gleiss Lutz](https://www.gleisslutz.com/en/know-how/new-eu-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content-impact-and-options)). California's SB 942, as amended by AB 853 and operative since 2 August 2026, is the stronger driver. According to one secondary source, the latent disclosure must "uniquely identify the content" ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/08/new-california-ai-disclosure-rules-become-operative)). The statute text itself was not checked.

Overall, ECC matters in practice for the identifier and manifest-recovery job and is marginal for pure AI-disclosure detection. The requester's header-plus-ID design maps directly onto that division.

## Bit accuracy hides the failures provenance cares about

Classical papers reported message-level outcomes as a matter of course. Examples include message error rates over 2000 images ([Miller, Doërr & Cox 2004](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1414/download/mm-doergw-040601.pdf)), the JPEG quality at which the first error appears ([Rey et al. 2003](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1360/download/mm-reych-030121.pdf)) and the chip rate needed for a BER target ([Bastug & Sankur](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1343/download/cm-bastah-040201.pdf)). The deep-learning era replaced these with bit accuracy, which is the main reason ECC looks unnecessary there. A mean of 99% says nothing about whether any single image returns its ID.

The benchmarks are built for detection. WAVES uses TPR at 0.1% FPR as its headline metric. It criticises bit accuracy for "insensitivity to error distribution", warning that "certain types of errors (like clustered errors) might be more detrimental" ([WAVES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08573)). W-Bench reports TPR at fixed FPR and states that baseline bit accuracies "do not include error correction methods, such as BCH" ([W-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18775)). MarkDiffusion uses TPR at 1% FPR and F1 ([MarkDiffusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10569)). SynthID-Image names the missing metric outright: "Bit accuracy does not explicitly measure what we might be interested in, which is how often we get the entire payload right, i.e., code accuracy". It notes that code accuracy "degrades more quickly with challenging transformations than bit accuracy", yet its published comparison table still reports only bit accuracy ([SynthID-Image](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09263)).

**No located benchmark requires exact recovery of a multi-bit ID.** Individual papers keep reinventing such a metric, often presenting it as new. InvisMark introduced a "decoding success rate" ([InvisMark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07795)). README introduced a "Zero Bit Error Image Rate", which rose from 1.2% to 86.3% for its 2048-bit signatures ([README](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04495)). Gaussian Shannon uses TPR at 100% bit accuracy as its primary metric ([Gaussian Shannon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26167)), NAWE reports word error rate ([NAWE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25972)), and the LLM papers report a "match rate" ([Qu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16820)).

Two results show how much bit accuracy conceals. SSL watermarking is one of the few papers to report word error next to BER: at JPEG 50, a 20.8% BER corresponds to a **98.9% word error rate** ([SSL watermarking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09581)). Under Gaussian Shannon's attack suite, `dwtDctSvd` returns its full payload in only **6–14% of images**, against about 0.97 for the LDPC-coded scheme ([Gaussian Shannon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26167)).

Wrong IDs, as opposed to failed decodes, are almost never reported. Yet that is the quantity a C2PA resolver or a legal attribution depends on, and the miscorrection result shows bounded-distance BCH generates them silently ([Li, Zenodo 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/22970405)).

Arithmetic on the published ChunkySeal figures shows the scale of the blind spot. Assuming independent errors, ChunkySeal's identity-transform bit accuracy of 99.74% gives exact 1024-bit recovery about 7% of the time. Its overall 99.15% gives about 0.02% ([ChunkySeal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12812); my arithmetic). Real errors are not independent, as the requester's data below shows. Whether neural extractor errors cluster by image or by bit index has not been measured in any source reviewed. It matters directly for interleaving and code choice.

## The proposed study is open, but only as a matched exact-ID benchmark

### No located paper covers more than a slice

The nearest works each cover part of the design and leave the rest open:

| Work | Codes | Backbone | Matched quality | Exact metric | Soft input | Known bits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NAWE (Sep 2026) | polar, CRC-aided list decoding | own classical carrier + neural denoiser | PSNR, but competitors keep their own coding | WER | yes | sync cells |
| Li, Zenodo (Sep–Oct 2026) | BCH, Hamming(7,4) | MBRS, 36 bits | payload-matched | yes | no | no |
| Gaussian Shannon (CVPR 2026) | LDPC + majority vote | in-generation diffusion, 256 bits | n/a | TPR at 100% bit accuracy | not stated | no |
| Gaussian Shading++ (2025) | PRC/LDPC vs BCH | in-generation diffusion | n/a | TPR, bit accuracy | yes | seed header |
| InvisMark (2025) | BCH | own model, 256 bits | n/a | success rate | no | no |
| **Proposed** | 5G LDPC, polar, LTE turbo, BCH, RS, repetition | `dwtDctSvd`, ChunkySeal 1024, Pixel Seal 256 | wPSNR | exact ID + wrong ID | logits / vote-fraction LLRs | header detection + pinning |

NAWE is the closest. It decodes CRC-aided polar codes from soft symbols and compares against TrustMark, SSL, Pixel Seal and WAM over 34 attack families. However, it "retains the systems' different payloads and coding" and concedes that "equal PSNR does not equalize information rate, coding gain, or perceptual visibility" ([NAWE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25972)). The Li study controls payload but tests only hard-decision BCH and Hamming on 36 bits. Its v2 also retracts a headline 4.2× effect as an artefact of the training mixture, which is a warning about how fragile such comparisons are ([Li, Zenodo 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/22970405)).

No located paper documents feeding a post-hoc neural extractor's logits into an LDPC or polar decoder at 256–1024 bits. None uses known header bits both for detection and as pinned bits inside the ID decode. ChunkySeal also removes the main excuse the survey gives for skipping ECC. Spending half of its 1024 bits on parity still leaves 512 information bits, more than any other open post-hoc model carries raw.

In coding terms, pinning known header bits is code shortening: known positions get infinite-reliability inputs. The idea itself is not new, and NAWE's known sync cells and BREW's designated-codeword check are close relatives ([BREW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00348)). What is publishable is the measurement: how much a known tag moves exact-ID and wrong-ID rates at a fixed false-positive rate on each backbone.

### What the requester's own measurements already show (requester data, not literature)

On `dwtDctSvd` at wPSNR 40, with 1024 coded bits and 100 images × 77 attacks, all rate-1/2 codes recovered about 97% of IDs with the colour-search decoder. LDPC and polar returned **0 wrong IDs**, against **1,160 wrong IDs uncoded** (about 15% of 7,700 trials). Read against the literature, code choice barely moves the success rate at this operating point but decides the undetected-error rate. That matches the miscorrection finding for bounded-distance decoders, and the BCH and RS wrong-ID counts are the next numbers worth extracting.

The ~3% residual is plausibly made of attacks that destroy or desynchronise the channel. The library's own README says the method is "not robust to resize or aspect ratio changed crop" ([invisible-watermark](https://github.com/ShieldMnt/invisible-watermark)). If raw BER on those attacks sits near 0.5, no code will recover them. That is a hypothesis to confirm per attack.

LDPC rate 1/2 beating rate 1/3 at fixed quality is the classical dilution mechanism Rey et al. describe. Coding theory nevertheless says repetition adds no coding gain under ideal soft combining ([Rey et al. 2003](https://www.eurecom.fr/publication/1360/download/mm-reych-030121.pdf)). A rate-1/2 win therefore signals information lost before the decoder: a few coarse votes per coded bit, or a per-bit SNR below the code's threshold. Two things follow. The gap should be checked against the cross-length strength noise already seen in the requester's strength sweep. And a finer soft metric, such as each block's distance to the quantiser boundary, would need extraction re-implemented outside the library, which the current library-only rule does not allow.

On ChunkySeal at its default strength (wPSNR 48.9), with 10 images × 18 moderate attacks, the mean was 6.4 bit errors per 1024 (median 3, maximum 60). If those errors were independent at that mean rate, all 1024 bits would be correct in about 0.16% of frames and a 256-bit slice in about 20%. The observed **13% and 42.5%** are far higher (my arithmetic). Errors therefore concentrate in a minority of bad frames. This is precisely the clustered-error regime that WAVES says bit accuracy hides, and it means code gains will depend on the per-frame error distribution rather than the mean.

LDPC rate 1/2 recovering 180 of 180 IDs is strong but statistically thin: the rule of three bounds the failure rate only below about 1.7% at 95% confidence. Even 0 wrong IDs in 7,700 `dwtDctSvd` trials bounds the wrong-ID rate only below about 4×10⁻⁴, far above the α = 1e-6 used for header detection.

### Five design choices decide whether reviewers see novelty

The first choice is operating points inside the window where codes differ. The classical thresholds give a map: BCH collapses above a raw BER of about 0.1, repetition takes over around 0.15–0.20, and codes with minimum distance below the code length cannot correct beyond about 0.25 ([Darbon et al. 2001](https://lre.epita.fr/dload/papers/darbon.01.ei.pdf)). Moderate attacks on ChunkySeal sit far below that window, and raising ChunkySeal's strength to match wPSNR 40 pushes it further from it. Reporting raw per-coded-bit BER for every attack, and sweeping attack strength into ChunkySeal's own cliff region, will separate the codes.

That region is where the interesting result lives. At the bit accuracies ChunkySeal reports for 55% crop (86.90%) and 10% brightness (83.62%), a memoryless hard-decision channel has a capacity of only about 0.44 and 0.36 bits per bit (my arithmetic using Video Seal's C(p)). That is below rate 1/2. Rate-1/2 success there must come from soft logits or from per-image error concentration, and that is a finding no prior paper can claim.

The second choice is to recognise that the rate trade-off differs by backbone. In `dwtDctSvd`, lowering the rate dilutes energy per coded bit. In ChunkySeal and Pixel Seal the raw interface is fixed, so lower rate costs payload, not image quality, and the classical r1/2-over-r1/3 result should not transfer. A fixed 512-bit message leaves ChunkySeal no rate below 1/2 and does not fit Pixel Seal's 256 raw bits at all. The fair-comparison rule therefore needs a per-backbone message length or a common shorter one.

The third choice is to report exact-ID rate, wrong-ID rate and detection TPR at a fixed FPR together. False IDs should be measured on a large unwatermarked null set, because decoders do not see uniform random words there. All of these need confidence intervals.

The fourth choice is to measure logit calibration and error correlation before trusting logits as LLRs. No source on extractor calibration was found. The LLM work had to engineer calibration to get usable LLRs ([CORE-BREW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24163)), and Luo et al. rejected LDPC precisely over noise estimation ([Luo et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04580)). Plain and normalised min-sum decoders ignore a global LLR scale, but sum-product and offset min-sum decoders do not, and over-confident wrong bits hurt all of them. A reliability plot of empirical error against logit magnitude, per attack, would answer the question directly.

The fifth choice is to differentiate explicitly from NAWE, the Li study, Gaussian Shannon and PRC, and to keep desynchronising attacks as a separately reported class. Codes cannot fix those attacks, and classical work handled them with templates.

Natural venues are coding-centred: IEEE WIFS, ACM IH&MMSec, ICASSP, IEEE TIFS or SPL. A benchmark-track version would add exact-ID metrics and ECC to an existing benchmark.

## Conclusion

The field's indifference to ECC is largely a product of its metrics. Bit accuracy and TPR at fixed FPR measure presence and average fidelity, both of which codes barely change. Exact identifiers and wrong-ID rates are what codes change, and almost nobody reports them. The deployed systems that actually need IDs (TrustMark, InvisMark, Digimarc) all code their payloads, yet the only public designs are hard-decision BCH, CRC and convolutional codes. Soft-decision modern codes on neural extractors are an engineering gap, not a solved problem.

The 1024-bit post-hoc models change the classical calculus. Without energy dilution, the old fixed-quality rate penalty disappears and the question becomes how to spend raw capacity. Per-frame error concentration then decides which codes win. A study that maps exact-ID and wrong-ID rates across that regime, with known-header decoding and measured logit calibration, would answer a question that C2PA leaves to vendors and that SynthID-Image names but does not publish. Its value depends on reaching the attack strengths where the codes actually diverge and on reporting the wrong-ID axis prominently. Coding novelty alone will not carry it, and with two related preprints in September–October 2026 and the CVPR 2026 LDPC paper, it is best done soon.
